## **4\. Application of Ethical Theories**

*Target: about 800 words. The brief is specific here: first explain precisely why the SE Code cannot resolve the dilemma, then apply **at least two theories to the same dilemma** and compare their conclusions. Don't describe the theories in general terms; apply each one to our own evidence.*

### **4.1 The dilemma the SE Code cannot resolve**

The ACM/IEEE Software Engineering Code of Ethics provides professional principles for SE engineers, but it is insufficient and ambiguous when commercial interests, public benefits and individual safety conflict. ReMeal’s Mystery Bag model illustrates this limitation. The element of surprise box encourages customers to purchase surplus food that might be otherwise discarded, which serves the employer and the waste goal. However, this model makes it difficult for customers to assess a bag’s suitability through allergen and specific food information. In our review of 38 Mystery Bag listings from 10 vendors, none of them showed allergen or halal information, and 10 did not indicate their contents (E18-E24). This creates tension with clause 1.03 and clause 1.04 on safety and disclosure of potential danger. Although ReMeal’s Term §5.3 mentions allergen-related limitations and allergic remark reminder, this information is buried in legal documents rather than clearly presented at the purchase page (E12, E13, E15), which clause 1.06 is silent for the visibility. Clause 1.02 requires engineers to balance competing interests, but it does not specify how to prioritise environmental and economic benefits against the potential health risks faced by consumers with food allergies. The Code identifies the relevant duties but cannot determine whether the model should continue or what risk level is acceptable. The bag model is also a business decision by management which is outside the engineer’s control.

### **4.2 Utilitarian analysis**

*Weigh the outcomes using our evidence: food rescued and money saved against allergic-reaction risk, food discarded at home (Zhang et al.; Yang and Yu, 2025), and what the coded reviews show.*
A major branch of Consequentialism. The morality of an action is determined solely by its outcomes or consequences; max net utility
\[Write here\]

Utilitarianism is a major branch of Consequentialism, judges an action by its consequences, favouring the option that produces the greatest good for the greatest number. The benefits are obvious: food is rescued, consumers pay less and vendors recover costs. A merchant using another similar Mystery Bag food platform said that it helps them pass savings to customers through rebates (CNA, 2025). These benefits support retaining the model if more surplus food is consumed. 

However, bags sold do not necessarily equal food saved. Zhang et al. (n.d) reported that consumers may throw away items in surprise bags they dislike, while Yang and Yu (2025) identified risks of overproduction and reduced full-price sales. A Fox News (2024) reported a vegetarian buyer discarding every meat item in her surprise bag, illustrating how waste may be transferred from a vendor’s kitchen to a customer’s home. Missing allergen information also creates potential health risks as undeclared allergens can cause severe reactions (Michelsen-Huisman et. al. , 2018). 

From a utilitarian perspective, Mystery Bags are justified only when their overall benefits outweigh the potential harm. ReMeal should therefore evaluate actual food consumption rather than simply counting bags sold as food saved (E07).

### **4.3 Deontological analysis**

*Apply duty and informed consent to the same dilemma: can a buyer consent to a bag they can't see? Apply the universalisability test to ReMeal's rule.*
The morality of an action is based on whether it follows a set of rules or duties
\[Write here\]

Deontology judges an action by whether it fulfils moral duties rather than its consequences. ReMeal takes payment as the vendor’s agent (Terms §1.1) has a moral duty to provide sufficient information for the customer to make informed decisions to safeguard consumers’ health. A customer may agree to receive unexpected food from the Mystery bag but this does not mean they have knowingly accepted undisclosed allergen risks. As a food allocating platform earning revenue from every sale, ReMeal should take reasonable steps to ensure the consumers are not exposed to avoidable risks. Although Term §5.3 disclaims clearly that the platform is not required to provide allergen information, it cannot remove its responsibility to communicate potential risks clearly at the purchase page, particularly for consumers who have food allergic or religious requirements.

Kant’s principle of treating people as ends in themselves further challenges withholding safety information to preserve the surprise element or protect sales. Consumers should not be treated merely as a means for ReMeal and vendors to recover costs or meet waste reduction targets. This concern is supported by our finding that none of the 38 Mystery Bag listings displayed allergen or halal information (E18-E24). A replacing item which is defaulted by checkout in the orders (E15, E26-E31) may contain ingredients or allergens the buyer did not expect. ReMeal therefore has a responsibility to provide clear allergen warnings, require vendors to disclose potential allergic information and obtain consumers’ agreement before replacing unavailable items, even if the food is sold in mystery bags.

Kant’s universalisability test further questions this practice. If every food platform withheld known allergen information whenever disclosure might affect the surprise element of Mystery bags, consumers could no longer rely on food platforms to provide essential safety information. This would undermine the trustworthiness for fair food transactions and could not be accepted as a universal moral principle. From a deontological perspective, reducing food waste and generating commercial benefits cannot justify neglecting consumers’ fundamental rights to safety and consent for choice. Therefore, ReMeal should maintain the Mystery Bag model only with minimum safeguards that fulfill these moral duties, regardless of whether doing so reduces its commercial benefits.


### **4.4 Comparing the conclusions**

|  | Utilitarianism | Deontology |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Verdict on Mystery Bags | The overall environmental benefit of reducing food surplus may be argued justified to the potential health problem and overproduction. | The duties as a food platform should be respected although the sales or convenience may decrease. |
| What ReMeal must do | ReMeal must evaluate actual food consumption instead of simply calculating sales. |  ReMeal must fulfil the moral duties to protect consumer health for charging vendors’ money. |
| Where they agree | Vendors recover the cost of surplus, consumers purchase food at a lower price and food is rescued. | Consumers have consent that they may receive unexpected food from Mystery bags. |
| Where they disagree | Potential overproduction and severe allergic reactions to the consumer. | Consumers’ consent of potential allergic or halal information is not protected as a customer. |

\[Write here: what the comparison reveals that neither theory shows alone. This is what the top grade band calls "adds insight".\]

Utilitarianism ensures environmental benefits and minimizes customer loss, whereas Deontology emphasises the moral responsibility of a food platform to safeguard consumer health. Both ethical theories complement each other by promoting stakeholder welfare while reducing potential environmental harm. Justifying the Mystery bag model does not mean that ReMeal should abandon it. Instead, operating the model within clear ethical boundaries can help ReMeal maintain its fundamental goal of reducing food waste in a more comprehensive way.
